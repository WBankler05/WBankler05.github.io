Welded Steel Truss

Predicting where a welded truss would fail, then loading it until it did.

Designed, MIG welded, and load-tested a steel truss as the prototype for a bridge reconnecting Nashville's Jefferson Street over I-40. It failed at 246.8 lb in the exact members and failure mode I predicted.

The challenge

Design, build, and test a steel truss to failure, and show that the analytical prediction of how and where it fails matches what actually happens. The truss was the prototype for a bridge that would cap Interstate 40 and reconnect Nashville's Jefferson Street community, which the highway split in two.

The truss spans 4 ft in four 1 ft panels with a 1 ft depth, pinned at A and on a roller at E, with a single load P applied at the top center joint C.

My role

  • Fabricated the truss by MIG welding the members to gusset plates.
  • Performed the structural analysis and calculated the yielding and buckling limits for each member.
  • Optimized the loading configuration to eliminate critical buckling failures in the weakest members.

Analysis

Solving the truss by the method of joints with the geometry above gives each support a reaction of P/2. Because the panels are square, the diagonals sit at 45 degrees, so:

  • The end diagonals (AH and EF) carry 0.707P in tension.
  • The central diagonals (HC and CF) carry 0.707P in compression, which makes them the members most likely to buckle.
  • The bottom chord members (HG and GF) carry P in tension, and the top chord carries P/2 in compression.
  • All three verticals (BH, CG, and DF) are zero-force members under this load case.

For each compression member I compared the load at which it would yield against its Euler buckling load, and the governing limit was buckling of a central diagonal.

Testing and results

We mounted the truss in a test frame and pulled down on the center joint through an inline digital scale until it failed. The two central diagonals buckled at 246.8 lb, which were the exact members and failure mode the analysis predicted.

The measured load was about 8% above the predicted 228.75 lb. The as-built diagonals were shorter than the design length, and a shorter member resists buckling better, which accounts for the truss carrying slightly more than predicted. Yielding would not have occurred until roughly 4,500 lb, so buckling governed by a wide margin, exactly as the analysis showed.
